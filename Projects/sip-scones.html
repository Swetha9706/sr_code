What is Sip & Scones?

What the atmosphere taught us about the design

Designing for a digital afternoon tea experience meant thinking beyond functionality. It meant understanding what makes afternoon tea special in the first place.

Early research showed afternoon tea isn't just a meal. It's a social experience, steeped in tradition and ambiance. A study by Lin and Chen (2022) found that 93% of afternoon tea patrons, mostly young women aged 24 to 29, choose a restaurant based on atmosphere first. That insight shaped our design direction. Space, mood, and pacing mattered as much as the food.

The design challenge

How do we introduce a digital ordering system without interrupting the ritual, aesthetic, or mood of the experience?

The Concept

A digital menu that stays out of the way

We envisioned Sip & Scones as a fine-dining restaurant with a tech-enhanced twist. Instead of kiosks at the entrance or ordering through personal phones, we placed a tablet-based digital menu discreetly at each table. Diners keep control of the ordering process. The setting keeps its elegance and intimacy.

The interface replicates the iconic three-tiered tea stand, allowing diners to:

The stand

Customize each of the three afternoon tea tiers: Sandwiches, Scones, Sweets

The pairing

Choose drink pairings from a curated menu

The pace

Review and complete their order at their own pace

The reward

Track loyalty rewards through a visual system of stacked teacups

Replacing traditional waitstaff with digital menus and robotic delivery carts struck a balance between efficiency and experience. Diners could enjoy a quiet, uninterrupted afternoon and still benefit from shorter wait times and fewer order errors (Bankar & Suresh, 2015).

Team reflection

“Our biggest concern was losing the essence of afternoon tea. We wanted technology that enhanced the experience, not one that disrupted it. The design needed to feel intuitive, elegant, and invisible.”

How does the ordering journey flow?

Mapping the journey, end to end

Before a single screen was built, the whole ordering journey was mapped in Figma: a guided path from the welcome screen through tier selection, drinks, an optional login, the loyalty reward, and payment. Branches stay shallow on purpose: the one real fork is whether a diner logs in to claim a reward or checks out as a guest, and it sits late enough that it never blocks the actual goal of ordering.

How was it actually built?

Making visuals behave like functional objects

LiveCode is a low-code environment, so most of the build was about wiring graphics to act as interface controls. Three decisions did the heavy lifting.

A taxonomy behind every tray

Each menu item was modelled as an image object tied to a defined name and a unique variable, then set visible on the tiered stand the moment it was selected. The tray you watch assemble in real time is really a set of images switching on and off.

One drink at a time

Drink selection used LiveCode's hilitebutton function to highlight the chosen pot and disable the rest, so a diner clicks off their current choice before picking another. The single-choice rule stays visible rather than firing an error.

A clean way to start over

Cancelling an order opened a confirm dialog wired to a switch-case: "No" leaves the order untouched, "Yes" resets the stack back to the first screen.

How was it tested?

Layout mattered more than expected

Paper prototypes are a useful tool for designers to develop and iterate on concepts in a low-cost manner. Before devoting time and resources to more complex prototypes, we tested a few initial interface options using paper prototypes to get user input early in the design process.

Paper · Wizard of Oz

The orders page mirrors the three-tiered stand used to serve English afternoon tea. Three buttons sit on the left: Tier 1, Tier 2, Tier 3. Click one and a dialog opens with the dishes available for that tier. Diners pick up to three dishes per layer, and click a dish again to remove it. Holding a dish opens a second dialog with its allergy and ingredient information. Clicking anywhere outside closes it. A recommended-drinks button sits top right. Continue, bottom right, moves on to drink choices and confirming the order.

Two concepts, compared

We tested two versions of the main ordering screen with several peers. The first keeps all three tiers on screen for the whole ordering process. The second zooms in on the tier being customised and fades the others out. We used Wizard of Oz testing, where a person manually stands in for the interface's responses.

The full-tier view felt more intuitive and satisfying

Peers preferred keeping all three tiers in view. It let them read their picks in one linear pass, instead of jumping tier to tier to check whether the choices paired well. Populating the tiers as they ordered also read as progress through the order, a form of visual storytelling shown to improve user experience (Li et al., 2020).

What did the tooling teach me?

Constraints shaped the product as much as ideas did

What the tool gave

Visuals as objects

LiveCode let visuals act as functional objects directly. Instead of hand-coding every part of a component, we could import a custom graphic and use the Property Inspector to get it working in a few lines of code. That made the harder parts of functional programming less intimidating, and more usable for a design-led project. We could also move objects around a card and see the final layout update live, without writing anything. A real advantage for designers working visually.

The tiered stand

The tiered tea stand benefited most. It was the thematic backbone of the whole concept, and it doubled as a visual progress indicator as diners built their order. Using imagery to show progress through a task is a known storytelling method for improving user experience (Li et al., 2020).

What the tool cost

A steep environment

LiveCode was also a hard environment to learn. The interface buried core functions, including the Project Browser (the tool for understanding a project's logic and configuration), inside hard-to-find menus. The syntax is simplified, but it still assumes foundational knowledge of how LiveCode models objects. Few tutorials exist outside the official documentation. Undo was limited to a single action, which made mistakes costly. This version of LiveCode asked for more functional-programming experience than we had as beginners. We should have spent more time learning the environment before building in it.

Prototyping backwards

We'd prototyped and tested with users early on. Even so, several planned features got cut or simplified in the final build, to keep the task flow from becoming cluttered. In hindsight, we approached prototyping backwards. We designed functionality before confirming we could actually build it in LiveCode. That cost us a system to store and recall variables, which turned out to be beyond our skill level at the time. We simulated what we couldn't fully build instead.

What did users actually do?

What we asked participants to do

Task script · round two, LiveCode prototype

"You have booked an afternoon tea experience for you and a friend at Sip & Scones… You arrive and are impressed by the atmosphere; a host seats you and presents a digital touchscreen tablet menu for you to place your order."

Task: Place and pay for an order using the digital ordering system. Your persona will be provided to you.

Key finding
Strong concept, minimal friction

Both users completed their tasks with only minor issues, and praised the visual design, ordering flow, and thematic fit. The design language read clearly as afternoon tea.

Gap identified
Switching menus felt rigid

Participants wanted to move more freely between food tiers and drinks to build better pairings. The fixed sequence felt limiting when they wanted to revisit an earlier choice.

Accessibility note
Onboarding needed clearer cues

Some asked for clearer instructions, especially for those whose first language isn't English. That is a prompt for more inclusive copy and visual guidance throughout.

Loyalty screen
Contextual guidance, not exploration

One participant needed the Help button to make sense of the loyalty screen, which validates built-in contextual guidance over leaving diners to explore their way to understanding.

If I built it again

What I'd change, given more time

Testing with real users surfaced gaps I wouldn't have caught alone. These are the changes I'd prioritise in a second iteration, ordered by user impact.

Menu switching & backtracking

Let diners move freely between the food tiers and the drinks menu, and back to change an earlier choice, instead of following one fixed sequence. Right now, revisiting a tier resets it: the on openCard function disables every option again, so changing your mind means starting the whole order over. Back buttons work well in ordering and shopping interfaces (Obendorf et al., 2007), and would need this iteration's missing piece: a system to store and recall variables.

Smarter dialogs

Replace LiveCode's standard dialog boxes with modal ones. Modal dialogs block every other action until closed (LiveCode, 2023a). Standard dialogs work fine functionally, but modal dialogs run as substacks, which means they can be styled beyond the default look, so dialogs would finally match the rest of the interface.

Visual checkout summary

Show the full tea tray and drink selections before payment, a moment to catch mistakes and see what's been built. Arrays could hold that data (LiveCode, 2023b) and, based on user feedback, extend to showing the actual selected imagery, not just a list.

Checkout validation

Prevent progress without at least one selection made. Right now nothing stops a diner from moving forward empty-handed; a future version should block that, keeping the order flow logical from start to finish.

Real sign-up flow

Add a real sign-up option on the log-in page, where diners trade their information for rewards. A loyalty system that collects customer data does two things: it brings diners back, and it gives the business insight to grow (Piccoli, 2008). Recalling login details would need the same kind of memory system as backtracking above, using an array-based data structure (LiveCode, 2023b).
