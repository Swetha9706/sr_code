What is Krētha?

A product that sits between informal tools and enterprise complexity

Krētha is an ongoing B2B retail procurement platform. It brings retailers, wholesalers, and manufacturers into one connected operational system instead of leaving purchasing scattered across WhatsApp, spreadsheets, phone calls, and memory. My role has been UX strategy, systems design, and implementation support, working directly with developers and business stakeholders as the product is built.

The design opportunity was a gap in the middle: informal tools are familiar but structurally weak, while ERP and CRM systems are too heavy, expensive, and rigid for smaller businesses. Krētha aims to be more structured than the first, and much lighter than the second, built for how small and mid-sized businesses actually work.

Who is this for?

Designing for the network around the order, not just the order

Krētha serves a full supply network rather than a single actor, so the design had to hold several roles at once, each with different needs, incentives, and levels of technical comfort.

Primary audience

Retailers

Kirana shops, specialty stores, pharmacies, and apparel sellers who need simple order placement, supplier discovery, repeat purchasing, and transaction history, without a steep learning curve or high cost.

Primary audience

Suppliers & Distributors

Regional wholesalers and FMCG distributors managing fragmented orders from multiple retail clients, who need centralised order intake and visible fulfilment status.

Secondary audience

Agents & Networks

Agents, brokers, and SME associations who facilitate platform adoption as trusted intermediaries, bringing retailers and suppliers onto the platform.

What problem am I solving?

Procurement here is rarely a single-screen task. It's scattered across people, channels, and time, with no shared record of what was agreed.

For many retailers, placing an order means comparing suppliers, checking stock rhythm, adjusting quantities, coordinating with staff, confirming changes, and making sure the final order still reflects business reality. When that happens across messaging threads, calls, spreadsheets, and memory, the result is slower ordering, avoidable errors, and weak visibility.

Fragmented, multi-channel coordination

Ordering spread across chats, calls, and spreadsheets means slower turnaround, avoidable errors, poor visibility, and weak auditability.

Repetition without infrastructure

Retailers reorder familiar products in recurring cycles, but with no system to support that, every order feels rebuilt from scratch.

Organisational complexity at small scale

Accounts need to represent businesses, not just individuals. That raises roles, permissions, approvals, and accountability, without overwhelming small operators.

How did I get to the problem?

I studied operational behaviour, not abstract personas

The central question wasn't "what screen comes next?" It was how procurement actually happens inside working businesses, and where it breaks down. That shifted the research toward workflows, dependencies, recurring actions, accountability, and exception handling.

How big is the opportunity, really?

India's B2B online marketplace is projected to become a major opportunity by 2030, supported by digital infrastructure growth and e-commerce momentum. But adoption among MSMEs depends on products being simple, trustworthy, and practical, not enterprise-heavy. That told me Krētha should fit naturally into how smaller businesses already operate, rather than imitate complex enterprise software.

Who is this really for?

Working with the business stakeholders, I pinned down who Krētha serves and what it has to be. Not a buyer-seller pair, but three distinct business actors, each entering the same order from a different side of the chain. It also has to carry business accounts, organisation details, and information shared across roles. That ruled out consumer-style assumptions and pointed straight at role-aware workflows and organisation-level structure.

Each one acts as a business, not an individual, so every account carries an organisation behind it.

Three findings that shaped everything after

Put the market picture next to the platform's scope, and the same three things kept surfacing. These are the read on the problem that the rest of the work is built on.

Procurement is collaborative

Even when one person places the order, decisions are spread across people and time. The platform had to support that coordination, not assume a single decision-maker.

Repeat ordering is critical

Reordering familiar SKUs in cycles is one of the strongest behaviours in retail. It deserves first-class feature design, not an afterthought.

Business users need control

Speed matters for high-volume ordering, but only when it keeps reviewability, auditability, and room to handle exceptions.

From findings to a mapped system

The findings gave me something to build from. Next I mapped the workflows, role relationships, and decision paths for the key procurement events: draft order creation, supplier review, changes, confirmations, and follow-up after an order. Mapping them surfaced the real constraints too. Backend dependencies, phased development, and permission complexity all shaped what the MVP could be.

It had to behave like a connected system, not a menu of tools

Krētha isn't a set of separate features. Order placement, purchase history, saved lists, account roles, subscriptions, and supplier interaction all shape one another. So the information architecture had to work as one connected system, not a menu of independent tools.

That view changed how I made decisions. An order stopped being a single transaction record. It became part of a lifecycle: intent, internal coordination, supplier response, revisions, and fulfilment. A purchase list stopped being a saved cart. It became a planning object tied to recurring buying, stock, and how efficiently a business runs.

Navigation and hierarchy followed the same logic. The design had to make relationships obvious: who belongs to which organisation, who can prepare or approve an order, which lists are personal and which are shared, and what each subscription tier unlocks. Clarity about those relationships mattered as much as a clean screen.

One lifecycle, not a series of isolated screens

Browsing, ordering, editing, approving, fulfilling, and reordering form a single lifecycle. Design decisions had to hold across the whole arc of a procurement event, not just one screen at a time.

What held it together?

Consistency as a product property, not a visual one

As the platform expanded, consistency became less about visual polish and more about operational predictability. Reusable components weren't just there to speed up design and development. They made different workflows feel familiar and trustworthy.

The system leaned on reusable form and table patterns for dense business workflows, shared interaction logic for status and permissions, modular structures for procurement and account features, and standardised patterns for bulk actions, decision states, alerts, and confirmations.

This matters because complex products become hard to trust when each section behaves differently. A coherent system lowers the learning curve, supports future expansion, and helps the platform mature from an early-stage tool toward a real operational environment.

Scalability extended beyond components, too. Navigation, entity relationships, and content density all had to support the product today while leaving room for future supplier workflows, richer fulfilment logic, larger organisations, and deeper business administration.

What did I learn?

Strong product design makes systems understandable, not screens look finished

Krētha taught me that strong product design is less about making screens look finished and more about making systems understandable. The most valuable work came from clarifying relationships, sequencing actions carefully, surfacing the right information at the right moment, and designing for the mess of real operational behaviour.

It also showed me the value of designing with implementation in mind. Technical constraints didn't lower the quality of the process. They sharpened it. They forced clearer priorities, more explicit assumptions, and more durable decisions about what the product needed to be now versus what it could become later.

A few lessons stuck. Real procurement flows need exception handling as much as ideal-path design. Draft states, supplier-side changes, and partial edits are where credibility lives. Repeat behaviour deserves first-class support, because it directly shapes efficiency. Multi-user products need early systems thinking around identity, permission, and coordination, not a retrofit onto an individual-user model. And scalability is usually an information-architecture problem before it becomes a visual one.

If the next phase grows, the strongest opportunities are deeper post-order fulfilment visibility, inventory-linked planning, stronger approval logic, and sharper team-administration tools. Those areas would keep tightening the link between procurement, operations, and business coordination.

Where is Krētha now?

Krētha is an active product in development, not a closed portfolio artifact. Its current state is shaped by live constraints and iterative delivery, and the platform vision keeps evolving around retailer, wholesaler, and manufacturer workflows. That's the honest status. This isn't a wrapped-up project with a tidy ending; the case study reflects the real nature of product work: proposing, testing, adapting, and improving the system through collaboration.
